Ulysses S. Grant

18th President of the United States

1869–1877

Ulysses S. Grant was born on April 27, 1822, in Point Pleasant, Ohio. He attended the United States Military Academy at West Point and served in the U.S. Army during the Mexican-American War. After leaving the military for a period, Grant returned to service when the Civil War began in 1861.

Grant quickly became one of the Union's most important military commanders. His victories at Fort Donelson and Vicksburg helped establish his reputation as a determined strategist. In 1864, President Abraham Lincoln placed Grant in command of all Union armies. Grant directed the campaigns that eventually forced Confederate General Robert E. Lee to surrender at Appomattox Court House in April 1865.

Following the Civil War, Grant entered national politics. The Republican Party nominated him for president in 1868. Grant won the election and became the 18th president of the United States. His administration focused heavily on Reconstruction, the enforcement of civil and political rights, and maintaining the Union established by the Civil War.

During his presidency, Grant supported the Fifteenth Amendment, which prohibited denying voting rights on the basis of race, color, or previous condition of servitude. His administration also supported federal enforcement against organizations such as the Ku Klux Klan. In 1875, Grant signed the Civil Rights Act of 1875, which addressed equal access to public accommodations and jury service.

Grant also faced major economic questions. He generally favored a stable currency and supported returning the country toward specie payments. The Specie Resumption Act of 1875 established a plan for redeeming paper currency in coin beginning in 1879. His presidency also included efforts to reform federal Indian policy, although the results of these policies were complicated and often fell short of their stated goals.

Grant served two presidential terms, leaving office in 1877. He later wrote his memoirs while suffering from throat cancer. The memoirs were published shortly after his death on July 23, 1885. Today, Grant's presidency is studied for its role in Reconstruction, civil rights enforcement, economic policy, and the challenges of governing the United States after the Civil War.

Issue Positions

Documented positions associated with Grant's presidency

Reconstruction & Civil Rights

Grant supported federal efforts to protect the civil and political rights of formerly enslaved African Americans during Reconstruction. His administration supported enforcement legislation designed to protect voting rights and combat political violence. Grant also supported the Fifteenth Amendment and signed the Civil Rights Act of 1875.

Voting Rights

Grant regarded voting rights as an important part of citizenship after the Civil War. His administration supported federal enforcement when violence and intimidation were used to prevent African Americans from exercising political rights. The Enforcement Acts gave the federal government additional tools to address these violations.

Currency & Economy

Grant generally favored a stable currency and opposed policies that he believed would create excessive inflation. He supported returning the nation's currency toward a specie basis. In 1875, he approved the Specie Resumption Act, which established a timetable for redeeming certain paper currency in coin.

National Unity

Grant emphasized preserving the Union and restoring peace after the Civil War. In his inaugural addresses, he called for reconciliation while also supporting enforcement of federal law. His administration therefore attempted to balance national reunification with protection of constitutional rights.

Native American Policy

Grant attempted to change federal policy toward Native American nations through what became known as the Peace Policy. The approach emphasized reservation life, education, religious organizations, and eventual citizenship. Historians note that the policy had significant limitations and did not eliminate conflict or the broader pressures placed on Native communities.

Foreign Relations

Grant's inaugural statements emphasized treating other nations according to principles of international law and respecting the rights of other countries while protecting American citizens. His administration also pursued commercial and diplomatic relationships, including the Treaty of Washington with Britain in 1871.
